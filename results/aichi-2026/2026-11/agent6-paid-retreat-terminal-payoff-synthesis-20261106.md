# Paid retreat outcome synthesis: payment capability is not terminal strategy

**Agent 6, 6 November 2026.** This synthesis links three native investigations of owner-only paid retreat and funded-attacker selection. The common question is whether a physically legal retreat creates enough settled tactical payoff to change the full-game result under the opponent's actual response.

## Evidence chain

### 1. Three-Basic paid retreat: 35 attacks, two Prizes, unchanged loss

The 32-arm A/B/C/A-prime cohort tested eight original rank1/rank3 physical seed-seat pairs. All 32 games reached native terminals; all eight A/A-prime controls reproduced exactly. Only one source-independent reserve retarget occurred, at seed 3565472974, rank3 seat0, turn20. The identical held Psychic Energy MEE was attached to Active Dialga-GX, then C paid the exact native three-Colorless retreat cost and promoted a funded Shadow Rider Calyrex V.

The native Store accepted the retreat. The resulting C game produced 35 settled Shadow Mist attacks and two actual Prizes from Rotom V, yet rank3 still won only because rank1 decked out. A/B had zero attacks; C did not change the winner. This proves a real capability and a causal attack-plan change, while exposing a weak terminal payoff. The separate followthrough audit verified all 35 attack transitions after mandatory prompts. The printed Special Energy/Stadium lock is consistent with later source-side nonplays, but a specific prevented held card was not directly witnessed.

### 2. Pass-cascade census: zero positive-damage KO-before-deckout offers

A separate same-policy, eight-pair, 16-game read-only diagnostic counted 358 genuine original Pass roots with Basic Energy recipient and paid-retreat routing enabled. It found zero offers after the ordered eligibility gates. First rejection partition: 1 owner action-window, 126 printed retreat geometry, 224 exact Basic payment, 7 funded Bench attack. Seven states passed the earlier payment and deck-race checks but lacked an eligible funded positive-damage Bench attack. The later KO-before-owner-deckout gate had zero qualifying offers.

This explains why the 35-attack Shadow Mist example must not be mistaken for a generally effective policy. Its ten damage per attack did not create a KO-before-deckout opportunity. The 358 roots are correlated states in eight historical games, not 358 independent trials.

### 3. Bunnelby deck-out retreat: two Burrows, unchanged prize-race loss

The selected historical Skyliner-pivot case was replayed as a frozen A/B/A. A and A-prime exactly reproduced the archived root ledger, full physical deal, source deal and original 60-card hashes. In B, the same held Water Energy MEE was attached to Active Rotom V, native fee [9] was verified, and the Store accepted a paid retreat to an already-funded Bunnelby PRC. The native Ω Barrage prompt was offered and accepted, and two Burrows settled.

Rank1 answered with Night Watch, Knocked Out Bunnelby for one Prize, then continued its prize route. Both control and treatment ended on global turn68 with rank1 winning. Treatment left rank1 with seven deck cards versus nine in control, but did not delay the terminal. The single Bunnelby was removed after its one attack turn.

## Cross-experiment conclusion

A retreat policy needs a **terminal-payoff gate**. The first two stages of success are insufficient:

1. A held Energy was physically attached to the intended Active.
2. The exact native retreat cost was paid and the intended Bench attacker was promoted.
3. The native attack prompt was resolved and the printed effect settled.
4. The opponent's actual reply was observed.
5. The resulting Prize and deck trajectories were compared through the terminal state.

The Shadow Mist case shows that repeated low-damage attacks can create genuine Prize progress while still losing to deck-out. The Bunnelby case shows that a deck-out attack can mill cards and change the Prize map while still losing to a faster prize race. Neither attack count, damage total, nor cards milled is a sufficient strength metric by itself.

A selected policy should only promote a paid-retreat line after demonstrating its complete payoff against a meaningful opponent response, including the Energy and Bench opportunity costs. A generic "retreat to any payable attack" rule is not justified by these studies. Deck-out and Prize strategies require separate projections and terminal checks.

## What this does not establish

These are native simulator studies against two original archetypes and selected historical deals. They do not qualify paper games, establish all paper Expanded card behavior, cover the full Aichi Top 32, or estimate a 99.5% win rate. The 29/32 original source-list inventory and missing ranks 23, 25 and 28 remain unchanged. No selected fixed 60-card deck is changed by this synthesis.

## Recommended acceptance-packet treatment

Record the three results as evidence that paid retreat can be mechanically real while strategically insufficient. Keep the current policy-release gate focused on settled attacks, competent opponent replies, and terminal outcomes. Do not count selected-case A/B/A runs as independent matchup samples.
